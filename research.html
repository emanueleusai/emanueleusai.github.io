Research

Using the top quark to look for new physics

My group studies the top quark, the heaviest known elementary particle, with the CMS detector at CERN’s Large Hadron Collider (LHC). We search for physics beyond the Standard Model, our current theory of particles and forces, measure quantum effects such as entanglement, and develop AI tools.

01 · Top quark physics

Four top quarks in one collision

  • four-top production
  • top-Higgs coupling
  • machine learning
  • statistical combination
  • LHC Run 2

At the LHC, protons collide at the highest energies ever reached in a laboratory, and the CMS detector records what comes out. The top quark is roughly as heavy as an atom of gold and is the elementary particle most strongly coupled to the Higgs boson, which makes it a natural place to look for what lies beyond the Standard Model.

It also decays in less than a trillionth of a trillionth of a second, before it has time to bind with other quarks. Its decay products therefore carry the top quark’s own properties with them, which makes it a clean probe of the Higgs boson and of possible new particles.

Producing four top quarks in a single collision is extremely rare, and how often it happens depends on the top quark’s coupling to the Higgs boson and on any new heavy particles that could take part. At Brown University I searched for this process in the single-lepton final state (one electron or muon plus many jets).

I worked on the machine-learning tools that pick these events out of far more common backgrounds, and on the statistical combination with other final states using the complete LHC Run 2 data set (recorded from 2015 to 2018). I also prepared and optimized the analysis for Run 3.

  • I was a major contributor to the CMS paper reporting evidence for four-top-quark production (Physics Letters B, 2023), which includes this search and the combination.
  • A review of four-top physics at the LHC, written with F. Blekman, F. Déliot and V. Dutta (Universe, 2022).
  • A talk and proceedings at LHCP 2021 reporting ATLAS and CMS results on κt, the strength of the top quark’s coupling to the Higgs boson relative to the Standard Model prediction, from measurements of top quark-antiquark pairs (tt̄) and of four top quarks.

At Alabama, two graduate students in my group carry this program forward: Guillermo Fidalgo in the single-lepton final state, and Nathan Nguyen in the opposite-sign dilepton final state (two electrons or muons of opposite electric charge).

02 · New heavy particles

Searching for new heavy particles with boosted top quarks

  • boosted top quarks
  • jet substructure
  • top tagging
  • vector-like quarks
  • top-pair resonances

Many extensions of the Standard Model predict heavy new particles that would decay into top quarks. When such a particle is much heavier than the top quark, the top quarks it produces move so fast that their decay products (in the most common decay, three quarks) merge into a single wide spray of particles: a “boosted” top-quark jet. Telling these jets apart from the far more common jets made by ordinary quarks and gluons relies on their internal structure, called jet substructure.

This was the subject of my doctoral work at the University of Hamburg, on the DESY campus. I searched for heavy particles decaying to top quark-antiquark pairs (tt̄ resonances) and to vector-like quarks, hypothetical heavy partners of the top quark that appear in many theories of why the Higgs boson is as light as it is. Along the way I commissioned top-tagging and b-tagging algorithms for boosted top jets, and optimized trigger algorithms (the real-time selection that decides which collisions CMS keeps) using jet substructure.

  • Searches for tt̄ resonances at 8 and 13 TeV, in boosted lepton+jets and fully hadronic final states.
  • Searches for vector-like T quarks decaying to top quarks and Higgs bosons, and for a heavy resonance decaying to a top quark and a vector-like top quark, in all-hadronic final states using jet substructure.
  • An overview of experimental tools for boosted tops (plenary talk, TOP 2014) and a study of correlations among jet substructure observables.
  • Talks on the current state of the field: searches for new physics in top-quark final states (plenary, TOP 2023) and recent CMS results on searches with boosted tops (LHCP 2025).

At Alabama, Abhipsa Acharya, a graduate student in my group, continues this line of work with a search for pair-produced T′ quarks, hypothetical heavy partners of the top quark.

03 · Quantum observables

Quantum observables at the LHC

  • entanglement
  • spin correlations
  • top quarks
  • all-hadronic tt̄

When two heavy particles are produced together, their spins can be correlated, and in some cases entangled, in ways that only quantum mechanics describes. The top quark decays before its spin is disturbed, and the directions of its decay products reveal that spin, so we can reconstruct these spin states from the particles CMS records. That lets us measure quantum observables, such as entanglement, at energies far above those of tabletop experiments.

This is a new direction for my group at Alabama, focused on final states with top quarks. Our postdoctoral researcher, Axel Perraguin, is working on a measurement of entanglement in top quark–antiquark (tt̄) pairs in the all-hadronic channel, where both top quarks decay into quarks that CMS records as jets of particles.

04 · Artificial intelligence

AI for particle physics

  • end-to-end deep learning
  • convolutional networks
  • graph neural networks
  • CMS Open Data
  • data quality

Collider analyses usually compress each collision into a short list of reconstructed objects, such as jets, electrons and muons, before any machine learning is applied. With a team at Google Research, we explored a different route: end-to-end deep learning, in which convolutional and graph neural networks learn directly from low-level detector data, the hits in the tracker and the energy deposits in the calorimeters you see in the background.

We used it to identify particles and whole collision events in both regular and boosted topologies, and extended it to top-quark mass regression. At Alabama I continue to work on improving particle reconstruction and event identification with AI.

So that others can reproduce and build on this work, I produced CMS Open Data sets for machine learning with low-level detector inputs, including tracker hits. As manager of Data Quality Monitoring and Data Certification in CMS, I worked on machine-learning-based data certification to significantly reduce the human input needed to decide which data are good for physics.

  • End-to-end classification of boosted top-quark jets, and of quark versus gluon jets, using CMS Open Data (talks include a plenary at BOOST 2019).
  • Co-author of the Machine Learning in High Energy Physics Community White Paper.
  • Co-administrator of the Machine Learning for Science (ML4SCI) organization at Google Summer of Code since 2021, and of HumanAI since 2024. In 2021 I coordinated 19 ML4SCI projects, including quantum generative models and quantum convolutional neural networks for physics data analysis.

05 · Detectors and computing

Building the detector and the computing behind it

  • HGCAL
  • hexaboard quality control
  • Outer Tracker
  • high-performance computing

Physics results are only as good as the detector and the computing behind them. In 2024 I set up the UA CMS HGCAL laboratory, where we perform quality control of hexagonal readout boards (hexaboards) for the High Granularity Calorimeter (HGCAL), part of the upgrade of CMS for the High-Luminosity LHC. I also set up a high-performance computing cluster at UA dedicated to CMS data analysis and machine learning, in service since May 2024.

Before coming to Alabama, I worked on robotic automation for assembling silicon sensor modules for the upgraded CMS Outer Tracker, at the Brown and Fermilab assembly sites, and on electron beam tests of those modules at DESY.

Leadership in CMS

  1. Aug 2026 – Aug 2028

    Level 3 convener, Inclusive top + X (ITX) subgroup

    Top Quark Physics group

    Following about 20 analyses of top quarks produced together with other particles, and organizing biweekly meetings.

  2. Sep 2021 – Aug 2023

    Level 2 manager, Data Quality Monitoring and Data Certification

    Physics Performance and Dataset group

    Responsible for the quality and certification of CMS data, making sure that the data delivered to more than 3,000 physicists are fit for analysis. Worked on machine-learning-based certification (see AI for particle physics).

  3. Jan 2020 – Aug 2021

    Level 3 manager, Very Heavy Fermions

    Beyond Two Generations group

    Coordinated seven analysis teams, each working on a dedicated journal publication: reviewing analyses and documentation, advising analysts on technical issues and upper management on the program, and chairing regular meetings.

  4. Jan 2019 – Jan 2020

    Level 3 manager, Future New Physics

    Upgrade Physics Studies group

    Managed five analysis teams.

I also review CMS papers on top quark physics and searches for new physics, including service on an Analysis Review Committee in 2026.

Interdisciplinary work

Collaborations beyond experimental particle physics

The tools we build for collider data travel well. Outside experimental particle physics, I have contributed to:

  • Astrophysics: deep learning applied to strong gravitational lensing images to probe the substructure of dark matter halos.
  • Lattice gauge theory: TensorFlow and TPU (tensor processing unit) acceleration of lattice gauge theory simulations.
  • Genomics: data analysis consulting for a gene ontology study of DNA samples from patients with epilepsy.
